## Knowledge base: information style

> This reference draws on ideas from "Write, Shorten 2025" by Maxim Ilyahov and Lyudmila Sarycheva. It is not a reproduction of the original text and is not a substitute for reading the book.

### Key concepts

**Red-flag words** — words and phrases that can be cut without losing meaning. Five categories: filler phrases, imposed judgments, clichés, inflated vocabulary, and vagueness. These aren't "forbidden words" but signals: "Attention — something might be off here."

**Information style** — a set of techniques for improving business writing. Core principles: usefulness, clarity, coherence, cleanness.

**Useful action** — the answer to "Why would the reader voluntarily read this?" The magnet that draws attention. The text's promise to the reader — to solve a problem or make them feel something.

**Text goal** — the author's inner understanding of what should change in the reader's mental picture after reading.

**Text tasks** — concrete techniques for reaching the goal. If the goal answers "where are we going?", the tasks answer "how are we getting there?"

**Cinematic sentences** — sentences that draw a picture in your head. Formula: "A person does this" instead of "The enactment of the process is as follows."

**Imposed judgments** — adjectives and adverbs expressing subjective opinion ("amazing quality", "best service") without evidence. They don't convince, because the reader can't verify them.

**Intensifiers** — a judgment stacked on another judgment: "absolutely free", "maximally profitable", "true professionals". They weaken rather than strengthen.

**Clichés** — phrases that travel from text to text unchanged: "team of professionals", "dynamic growing company", "personalized approach". Each cliché hides a concrete, more interesting meaning that needs to be unpacked.

**Bureaucratese** — the stilted style of government and corporate documents, leaking into ordinary business speech. Tells: nominalizations, passive voice, stacked possessives, the point hidden behind justification.

**Farmer approach (research-based)** — the topic is given; the material must be gathered: research, interview experts, verify data, write the piece. Expensive, but yields quality.

**Hunter approach (resource-based)** — working with ready-made material: the company's training materials, customer reviews, stories from sales reps. Fast and cheap, but quality depends on the source material.

**Module** — a self-contained block of text with a heading, devoted to one topic. Readable independently.

**Reader agreement** — an explicit statement at the start of a long text: what it's about, how it's structured, what the reader will get, what caveats apply.

**Slide as a capsule of meaning** — a slide should contain everything relevant to its headline and nothing else.

**Positioning** — the answer to "Who is the buyer and what benefit does the product give them?"

**The "therefore" test** — a method for checking whether promises are meaningful. Formula: "We do X, THEREFORE the customer gets Y." If "therefore" is empty — the claim is empty.

**News hook** — an event or fact that makes a press release newsworthy. Seven categories: threat, group pride, novelty/potential, solving mass problems, celebrity, taboo, absurdity.

### Deep dives by topic

#### Red-flag words

- Five categories: filler phrases, imposed judgments, clichés, inflated vocabulary, vagueness
- Removal ≠ prohibition: the same word can be essential in one context and useless in another
- Simpler text helps the reader solve their task faster — plumbing instead of a well

#### Filler phrases

- "Obviously", "as everyone knows" — either explain, or delete; don't pass off a debatable point as common knowledge
- "In my opinion" — only useful when contrasting with someone else's opinion
- "To be honest" / "frankly" — implies the rest was dishonest
- "Firstly, secondly" — in writing, replace with paragraphs or a numbered list
- Parentheses say "this doesn't matter" — either cut the content, or pull it out of the parentheses
- "By the way" — if it's relevant, don't flag it; if it's not, delete it
- Practical test: lots of commas piling up? Something's wrong — sort it out

#### Vagueness

- "More than 20,000 customers" → "20,000 customers" — the reader doesn't need audit-grade precision
- Large exact numbers ($10,543,768) → round to readable format ($10.5 million)
- Rankings ("in the top twenty") → name the exact position and explain why it matters
- Vague pronouns ("some", "certain", "various") — specify or cut

#### Inflated vocabulary

- "Demonstrate" → "show", "optimize" → "improve", "utilize" → "use", "facilitate" → "help"
- Pop-psych terms ("narcissist", "gaslighting", "trauma-bonded") used casually are both inflated and often inaccurate
- Important distinction: inflated vocabulary for show vs. precise technical term where it matters

#### Imposed judgments

- The "amazing pizza" problem: telling a friend the food was amazing doesn't convey the experience. Same goes for ad copy — adjective-based persuasion fails
- How to spot a judgment: a quality the author uses to describe their own subjective impression
- Four replacement strategies: (1) facts, (2) a scenario from the reader's life, (3) visual demonstration, (4) a real story
- "Facts of greatness" — numbers that matter to the company but say nothing to the reader ("Founded in 1987. 340 employees. 12 offices worldwide.")
- Test: cover the company name — if the text fits any company, it's empty

#### Clichés

- The "pantry staple" metaphor: clichés sit ready-made in your head, habitual to reach for, but they hide imprecise thinking
- Every cliché hides a specific meaning: "I don't want to work for the man" means different things to different people — unpack it
- Corporate clichés: "team of professionals" = "we've done this before"; "dynamic growing company" = "we just launched"; "industry-leading" = we have no specific claim to make
- Clichés in greetings and announcements: ask an AI to draft one — then avoid everything it produces
- Time parasites: "nowadays", "in today's America" — the present tense is the default; delete
- Way to detect a cliché: swap one word — if it breaks, it's a cliché

#### Write about people and events

- Formula: "Phenomenon is characterized by" → "A person does this"
- Nominalizations → verbs: "conducts an investigation" → "investigates"
- Passive → active: "a decision was made" → "the committee decided"
- Rebuilding algorithm: (1) is there an action, (2) who performs it, (3) phrase it clearly, (4) is there a conclusion
- State verbs ("is", "appears to be", "is located") — check whether they're hiding a real action
- Not everything needs an agent: "It's about to rain" doesn't need meteorologists as the subject

#### Don't overload sentences

- Main character and plot: figure out who the sentence is about, and make only them the subject
- The "cursor-backward" problem: the author holds the whole plot in their head and piles on qualifiers; the reader sees it all at once
- Parallel items: if they're synonyms — keep one; if they're different things — consider a list
- Chronology: narrate events in the order they happened; don't jump back mid-sentence
- Nested subordination: more than one level deep becomes dangerous
- Paired conjunctions ("not only… but also", "both… and"): the reader holds the first half while reading the second, adding cognitive load

#### Sentences in paragraphs and lists

- Paragraph structure: topic sentence → development → conclusion
- One paragraph = one topic; don't start on one thing and drift to another
- First-sentence test: readers scan first lines — they must work on their own
- Weak paragraph openers: "For example,", "However,", "In this case," — signs of lost autonomy
- Paragraph length: 3–9 lines is optimal; one-line paragraphs only as emphasis among long ones
- Lists: useful for parallel items; each item must match the format
- List order: consider the reader's search scenario — put the identifier first ("Children under 12 — children's rate")

#### Useful action, goal, and tasks of the text

- Useful action = magnet for the reader. Goal = compass for the author. Tasks = route
- Two kinds of useful action: solve a problem (pragmatic) or make them feel something (fear, outrage, pride, curiosity, nostalgia, confidence, joy)
- "To inform" doesn't work: readers don't want information for its own sake
- You can't write text "for everyone": the wider the audience, the harder to create text that grabs
- How to understand what readers need: observation (support tickets, forums, search queries), research (interviews, surveys), testing hypotheses, intuition

#### How to organize a text

Six structures:

1. **News / event** — inverted pyramid: what happened → details → backstory
2. **Story** — hero + problem + actions over time; real events, real people
3. **Instructions** — chronological sequence of steps; context/overview before details
4. **List / ranking** — parallel modules with consistent structure; informative subheadings
5. **Situation overview** — multiple thematic angles on one subject
6. **Argument** — thesis + evidence; linear or pyramidal reasoning

General principles:

- For long text — a reader agreement: what kind of text it is, how it's organized, what they'll get
- Checkpoints: subheadings break the text into accessible modules
- Content over form: don't force material into an unsuitable structure

#### On introductions and conclusions

**Introduction:**

- Don't start with banal truths ("Everyone knows what it's like to be sleep-deprived")
- Strategies: open with unknown facts, personal experience, or an immediate solution
- Weak openers: off-topic metaphor, broad generalizations, manipulation, "Great news!"

**Conclusion:**

- The "flip" move: if the article was objective — end with a personal opinion; if it had an opinion — end with statistics
- Options: call to action, further reading, a pitch for your services (directly and confidently), a recap of key ideas from a new angle

#### Tone

- Components of tone: choice of topics, attitude toward self and others, empathy, erudition and life experience
- Tone and platform: on social media — relaxed; in support chat — clear and warm; but the base principles are the same
- Fakeness is the worst tone problem. It shows up when the author pretends to be someone else or hides commercial interest behind flattery
- The best tone is your own: "Be yourself and talk as equals"

#### Depth and range

- Pick one audience and write for them; don't try to serve beginners and experts at once
- The worn-topic paradox: if something's on the internet, that doesn't mean the reader has seen it. Don't avoid familiar ground
- Levels of depth: (1) surface statement → (2) explanation with examples → (3) counter-example/opposing view → (4) nuanced analysis
- Examples are the main tool for depth: abstract terms need concrete manifestations
- The "flip the angle" approach: instead of "how to do it right", explain why people don't follow obvious advice

#### Genre: text about yourself

- Job application: answer each requirement point-by-point. Formula: "I can do X, here's the proof." If you don't match, say so directly
- After the required answers — a "free program": education, hobbies, personal details. But at the END, not the beginning
- Common mistakes: (a) Drama — tragic backstory as motivation; (b) "From the dawn of time" — starting with school years; (c) Fan letters — gushing love for the company; (d) "I need to stand out" — forced creativity
- Personal site: start with "Who are you and how are you useful?" in plain words. Then answer potential clients' questions
- Dating metaphor: the reader has 50 tabs open and is hunting for the fastest problem-solver

#### Genre: text about a company

- Opening: name + generic term ("software studio", "supplier", "training center") + primary benefit. No metaphors, no creative hooks on the "About" page
- Structure: intro paragraph → answers to reader questions in thematic blocks → contact information
- Mission and values: formula — "We care about X, THEREFORE we do Y and DO NOT do Z." If the value doesn't affect behavior, it's empty words
- "Which means" test: translate industry jargon into reader benefit. "ISO 27001 certified — which means your customer data is handled to international security standards"
- Acronyms and certifications: always explain what they mean FOR THE READER

#### Genre: work documents

Method (5–15 minutes):

1. **Group into subsections with headings** — like cleaning a room: same things, just sorted
2. **Rebuild within each section** — conclusion FIRST, chronological backstory LATER
3. **Pull key points into a summary at the top** — the manager reads 5 bullets and understands the situation
4. **Add a request / ask** — immediately after the summary: "Need your decision on X" or "No action required, FYI"

- The problem with bolding mid-paragraph: bolded words don't tell a coherent story when scanned. Bold = subheadings only
- Tables, charts, and diagrams attract more attention than body text

#### Genre: press release

- Seven categories of news hooks: (1) threat to life/health/well-being, (2) group pride, (3) untapped potential/novelty, (4) solving mass problems, (5) celebrity, (6) taboo, (7) sheer absurdity
- Moves to strengthen a weak hook: add uniqueness ("first in the US"), bring in a known figure, frame as an achievement against odds
- Structure: headline → first paragraph with ALL essential info (WHAT + WHY + WHERE/WHEN) → body with details → spokesperson quote → call to action → background
- Spokesperson quotes: must be vivid and quotable. If legal gutted the quote, cut it — a bland quote is worse than none
- Event announcements: date (with day of the week), time, and location — at the top

#### Genre: cold email

Structure (2–5 paragraphs max):

1. **Personal opening** — show that the email is written to this specific person
2. **Relationship history** — find a point of contact: met at an event, mutual connection, read their blog
3. **Acknowledgment of possible error** — use "maybe", "not sure". Owning uncertainty shows level-headedness
4. **Simple next step** — propose the MINIMUM-cost action for the recipient
5. **Bonus: work done in advance** — bring something concrete: a mockup, audit, sample edit
6. **Focus on reader benefit** — not "I graduated with honors", but "I noticed you need X and can help with Y"

Common mistakes: boilerplate ("mutually beneficial cooperation", "synergy", "leverage"), fake urgency, forced enthusiasm, too many details

### Key frameworks

**Strategies for replacing judgments:**

1. Facts — numbers, names, terms verifiable in the contract
2. Scenarios — "Imagine you're in this situation…"
3. Demonstration — show visually (photo, video, 3D render)
4. Stories — real cases from experience

**Clear sentence algorithm:**

1. Is there an action? (Is someone doing something?)
2. Who performs the action? (Are they the main character?)
3. State the action clearly (words understood? picture formed?)
4. Is there a conclusion? (Clear without extra words?)

**Paragraph construction:**

1. First sentence = topic statement (key words as early as possible)
2. Middle sentences = development
3. Last sentence = conclusion + bridge to the next paragraph
4. Each paragraph stands alone — readable without the previous one
5. Optimal length: 3–9 lines

**Landing page architecture:**

1. Introduction — show the product, name it, state the positioning and key benefit
2. Arguments — 3–5 "floors", each with a large illustration + explanatory text, proving one advantage
3. Details — secondary properties, FAQ, specs, testimonials
4. The deal — price, what's included, call to action

**Presentation slide checklist:**

1. Define the audience and their interests
2. Choose what to show in response to their question
3. Decide: speaker support or standalone reading?
4. Each slide = one self-contained capsule of meaning with its own headline
5. Key slides have a visual anchor (chart, photo, screenshot, diagram)
6. Clean up the text
7. Add conclusion/summary slides (at the start or end)

**Job application structure:**

1. State which role and company you're applying for
2. Answer each requirement point-by-point with evidence
3. If you don't match a requirement — say so directly
4. "Free program" — extra facts, hobbies, personal details (at the END)
5. Contact information

**Work document reorg (4 steps, 5–15 minutes):**

1. Group content into sections with descriptive subheadings
2. Within each section, conclusion first, backstory after
3. Pull key points into a summary at the top
4. Add a request or required action immediately after the summary

**Cold email framework:**

1. Warm-up (a personal detail showing this isn't a mass send)
2. Reader benefit (what you can solve for them)
3. Acknowledgment of possible error ("I might be wrong")
4. Something done in advance (mockup, sample, audit)
5. Simple next step (minimum effort for the recipient)
6. Contact info

**Press release structure:**

1. Headline (with a reader hook, not corporate jargon)
2. First paragraph: what happened + why it matters + where/when
3. Body: details, context, specifics
4. Spokesperson quote (vivid and quotable — or none)
5. Call to action
6. Background on the company
7. Press contacts

**Company values check:** "We care about X, THEREFORE we do Y and DO NOT do Z." If there's nothing to put after "therefore", the value is empty.

### Illustrative examples

**Cleaning company website:** Inflated version ("Cleaning is a true Art!") vs. concrete ("helps tidy apartments, offices, and homes"). Illustrates the core difference between author-centered and reader-centered text.

**Building notice:** A formal bureaucratic post ("Dear Residents: please be advised of a scheduled interruption…") vs. a humane version with date, time, duration, and a reminder to close valves. Same effort, completely different usefulness.

**The "amazing pizza" problem:** Telling a friend the pizza was amazing doesn't convey the experience. Parallel to advertising: adjective-based persuasion doesn't work.

**The "Pentagon mechanics":** Adding "Pentagon" to "mechanics" turns a dry technical phrase into a scene from a political thriller. Shows how word choice creates different mental "movies".

**"The cat sat on the mat":** A simple sentence progressively loaded with legal qualifiers — "The aforementioned feline, in accordance with standard feline behavioral patterns…" — until subject and verb end up a page apart. Shows what bureaucratese does to meaning.

**"I don't want to work for the man":** This cliché means different things to different people: creative freedom, flexible schedule, bigger paycheck. Unpacking the cliché reveals the actual specific meaning.

**Newsletter about 50 new features:** Instead of one email with a giant list — segmented emails to each user group about the features relevant to them.

**Press release: industrial controller:** A niche industrial product becomes broadly interesting through reframing: "A US manufacturer has launched production of spatial controllers for robots and autonomous vehicles" — a hook anyone can read.

**Cold email to a video creator:** A case study of the six-element framework: personal reference to the recipient's recent video, specific offer, admitting unfamiliarity with their editing process, sample trial edit done in advance, simple next step, contact.

**Quarterly special projects report:** Opens with four summary bullets (plan 85 % complete, Q2 overperformed, margin up, operations stable), then sections with subheadings. The manager reads the bullets in 30 seconds.

**Interview on winter driving:** An author with driving experience asks the expert follow-up questions a non-driver wouldn't: "What if I keep my distance and someone cuts in?" Shows how personal experience creates depth when working with experts.

### Core principles and guidance

#### On text quality

- Text is a small part of what determines a product's success. Don't expect word-level edits to open every door
- Good writing requires work on all levels at once — from word choice to overall purpose
- Start with the reader: "What do they need?" rather than "What do I want to say?"

#### On the work process

- Define the useful action before starting. This prevents endless revision cycles
- Segment the audience. Text "for everyone" is text for no one
- Fill the text with concrete examples from the reader's world. Abstractions without examples are white noise
- If you're stuck — the problem is usually higher up: unclear audience, unclear goal, insufficient research

#### On judgments and persuasion

- Stop persuading with adjectives. Show, don't tell
- The reader decides what's good. Your job is to give them material to judge from
- Demonstration (visual, experiential) is stronger than any text

#### On bureaucratese

- Institutions can and should write as a service. Clear text reduces errors, rework, and frustration — that's economics, not aesthetics
- Any document can be improved by putting the answer up front and the justification at the end

#### On writing in a changing media landscape

- Text is broader than "words on paper": it's the idea behind any communication — video, meme, game, photograph. Writing skills apply to all structured communication
- AI takes over templated and monotonous text generation, but it can't replace understanding of the reader's needs, feel for context, and the relationship between author and audience
- The core skill remains: understand what the reader needs, and express it clearly
